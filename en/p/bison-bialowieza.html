Białowieża caught between tank ditches and border walls

14 November 2026 · Poland's 'East Shield' military plan on the border with Belarus

Border fences and military fortifications split Europe's last primeval forest, isolating its herds and predators, while decades of drainage leave it ever more fragile.

Testimony of the European bison

I am the European bison, the heaviest wild animal that walks this continent. Białowieża is my last home. It was here that humans brought me back from the brink about a century ago, and here that I graze the clearings, strip bark in winter and open paths that everyone else in the forest later follows.

In 2022 the humans on the Polish side raised a steel wall 5.5 metres high and 186 kilometres long, crowned with razor wire, then fitted it with motion sensors and some 1,300 cameras. On the far side stands an older Belarusian fence. Between the two lies a strip of about 35 square kilometres where an animal sometimes ends up trapped, like someone who has walked into a room with no door.

We have become two herds that never meet: about 870 on the Polish side, according to the State Forests, and 690 on the Belarusian side, where the ideal number is just 400 and no predator keeps us in check. As for the lynx, my shy neighbour, its tracking collars used to log it crossing the border up to fifty times a year. Now the wall stands in its way, and scientists fear isolated lynx will be left to breed with their own kin.

Now humans speak of an 'East Shield': fortifications along 700 kilometres of their eastern frontier, which conservationists say could bring concrete bunkers, minefields and anti-tank ditches into the forest. And the forest is already tired. More than 700 kilometres of drainage ditches and straightened rivers have turned once impassable swamps into dry land you can stroll across.

The irony is that scientists at the Polish Academy of Sciences propose the opposite: restore the bogs, rivers and dense woods as natural barriers, because, as one of them puts it, a tank can't get through a place like this. The authorities have set aside $120 million of EU money for nature-based defence, but the work is slow and leans heavily on volunteers. I know nothing about armies. I do know that the swamp that stops a tank is the same swamp that gives me water.

What happened

Poland is pressing ahead with its East Shield programme to fortify its eastern border, while scientists and conservationists warn of damage to the Białowieża Forest World Heritage site and propose restoring bogs and rivers as natural barriers.

Chain of impact

  • A 186-km steel wall has split the forest since 2022
  • Bison herds and lynx populations are cut off on either side of the border
  • Genetic isolation pushes lynx towards inbreeding
  • East Shield fortifications could add ditches and concrete to a forest already drained by canals

Open question

Could restored wilderness be a sturdier line of defence than the concrete that tears it apart?
