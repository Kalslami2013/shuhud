🐟 Thornback ray · Southern North Sea Marine Protected Area, off Norfolk, UK

A 1.5-tonne Attenborough face guards the North Sea floor

19 November 2026 · An Attenborough boulder in a protected area still open to bottom trawling

On 12 November Greenpeace lowered a 1.5-tonne boulder bearing naturalist David Attenborough's face into the Southern North Sea protected area, which logged 37,200 bottom-trawling hours in 2025, nets that churn the sand where thornback rays hunt and lay eggs.

Testimony of the Thornback ray

On 12 November 2026, Greenpeace UK lowered a 1.5-tonne limestone boulder carved with the face of British naturalist Sir David Attenborough onto the floor of the Southern North Sea Marine Protected Area, off Norfolk in eastern England. It is the tenth stone in a barrier meant to obstruct bottom trawling, Mongabay reports. The UK Marine Management Organisation (MMO), which had threatened legal action on 5 November, logged its position the same day and says it is investigating. The environment department, Defra, said activities at sea should be lawful and consider other sea users' safety.

I am the thornback ray, living on the sand and gravel floor of this protected area. Surveys for the Norfolk Vanguard wind farm recorded me here, and its assessment classes the area as a nursery ground for my young. I feed on crabs, shrimps and small fish over the sand, and females lay leathery egg cases on this same seabed, on average 48 to 74 a year. None of us matures before age seven or eight.

The protected area, about 37,000 square kilometres, was designated for harbour porpoises, not for the seabed, so bottom trawling is still permitted. Bottom trawlers logged more than 37,200 fishing hours here in 2025, according to Greenpeace's analysis of Global Fishing Watch data; Oceana UK put it at 30,936 hours. On drop day, campaigners tracked 14 bottom trawlers nearby. Each pass of a net churns the sand I hunt in and sweeps over the layer where egg cases lie until they hatch.

Greenpeace says the ten boulders may not stop vessels but add obstacles, effectively closing 116 square kilometres. The government proposed in June 2025 to ban bottom trawling in 41 protected areas; this one is not among them and has been left to a later stage with no date. Until a decision comes, most of the seabed where our young live stays open to the nets.

What happened

The UK Marine Management Organisation says it is investigating the boulder drops and has not independently verified all ten positions, while the Southern North Sea protected area remains outside the June 2025 proposal to ban bottom trawling in 41 protected areas.

Chain of impact

  • The protected area was designated for porpoises, not the seabed, so bottom trawling stayed legal
  • Trawl nets churn sand and gravel, disturbing the crabs and shrimps rays feed on
  • Egg cases lying on the seabed stay in the nets' path until they hatch
  • Late maturity (7–8 years) makes losses slow to replace
  • The ten boulders obstruct trawling over only 116 km², per Greenpeace, while a government ban is deferred

Open question

Can a boulder on the seabed turn a line on a map into real protection, or is the real barrier a regulatory decision still not taken?
