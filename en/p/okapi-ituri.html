🦒 Okapi · Ituri province, eastern DR Congo

Ituri's gold, Ebola and the okapi's last forest

14 November 2026 · DR Congo's Ebola outbreak (Bundibugyo strain) passes 11,000 cases

An epidemic that most likely began in a gold-mining town feeds on the same movement that is digging up the Ituri forest: roving mines, armed groups and a shrinking forest.

Testimony of the Okapi

I am an okapi. Humans call me the 'forest giraffe', though I look down on nothing from above the trees. I live alone in the shade of the Ituri forest, stripping leaves with my long tongue, and almost no one ever sees me. In the reserve that bears my name, some 14,000 square kilometres, about five thousand of us lived in 1994. Then came the gold.

The World Health Organization says Ebola most likely began in Mongbwalu, a mining town in Ituri of about 130,000 people, most of them poor gold panners who work shoulder to shoulder in muddy pits and then move on from one mine to the next. Humans have not yet said how the virus crossed into its first body. By 11 November the country had 11,114 confirmed cases and 5,433 deaths across eight provinces, 7,728 of the cases in Ituri alone. The WHO says 400 to 500 new cases still appear every week, in rural villages that are hard to reach.

I know that movement well. My reserve holds at least 18 mining sites and between 15,000 and 25,000 miners, according to the IUCN's 2025 assessment, which blames artisanal mining for 98 per cent of our decline since 2009. Some 1,890 hectares of forest were lost in 2023 alone, along the river and the road. Humans dig the earth for a metal no one can eat, then call what remains of it a road.

The armed groups walk the same trails. The UN's Ebola coordinator says fighters of the Allied Democratic Forces are killing more people these days than Ebola is, and that a single attack in North Kivu killed 66 civilians, three health workers among them.

My rangers now hand out masks and gloves instead of chasing poachers. This virus does not infect me, but each time my protectors are busy surviving, I lose the people who guard the forest.

What happened

Confirmed Ebola cases (Bundibugyo strain) in DR Congo passed 11,114, with 5,433 deaths, as of 11 November 2026, most of them in Ituri; the WHO says 400 to 500 new cases are being recorded each week in rural areas hit by armed-group attacks.

Chain of impact

  • A gold rush draws thousands of mobile miners into the Ituri forest
  • Crowded, muddy pits without sanitation speed Ebola's spread
  • Forest clearing along the river and the road shrinks the okapi's habitat
  • Armed groups attack villages, health workers and reserve rangers
  • Rangers diverted to the epidemic leave the forest exposed to poaching and mining

Open question

Can an epidemic that most likely began in a gold mine be contained while the gold keeps digging into the forest?
